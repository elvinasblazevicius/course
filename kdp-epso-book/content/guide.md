# How to Use This Book
Welcome. Over the next few weeks you will read about fictional regional authorities, count dots in little squares and convert Swiss francs into euros. It is less glamorous than the career you are aiming for, but it is the door to it, and this book is designed to get you through that door with as little wasted effort as possible.

This workbook is built around one idea: you improve fastest when you practise under realistic conditions and then study every mistake. Each of the 413 questions in this book is original and has a full worked solution, so a wrong answer becomes a lesson rather than a mystery.

## What is inside
- **Part I — Understanding the tests.** What each test measures, how the questions are built, and proven methods for each question type.
- **Part II — Practice sets.** 65 verbal, 75 numerical and 75 abstract reasoning questions, graded from foundation to advanced, plus 24 situational judgement scenarios, 30 accuracy & precision questions and 24 prioritising & organising questions.
- **Part III — Three timed mock exams.** Each mock combines 20 verbal, 10 numerical and 10 abstract reasoning questions.
- **Part IV — Answers and worked solutions.** A quick answer key for every set, then a full explanation of every question, including why each wrong option is wrong.
- **Back matter.** A quick-reference card of formulas and traps, a skill index for targeted practice, a score tracker, a four-page error log and answer sheets.

## A simple routine that works
1. **Learn the method first.** Read the Part I chapter for a test type before you attempt its practice set.
2. **Practise untimed, then timed.** Work through the foundation questions without a clock and concentrate on method. Start timing yourself from the intermediate questions onwards.
3. **Mark immediately, review deeply.** Check your answers straight after each block. For every error, read the full solution and write one line in the error log: *what did I do, what should I have done, and what will I check next time?*
4. **Re-attempt.** A week later, redo the questions you got wrong without looking at your notes.
5. **Simulate the real thing.** Sit the mock exams in one go, at a desk, with the suggested time limit and no interruptions.

> **Tip.** Accuracy comes before speed. Candidates who rush early practice build bad habits that are hard to unlearn. Get the method right, and speed follows.

> **Coach’s note.** Nobody has ever regretted keeping an error log. Plenty of people have regretted making the same mistake for the fourth time, usually on test day.

## How the solutions work
- Under every practice question you will find the page of its worked solution, and every solution points back to its question.
- **Verbal:** each option is marked *Correct* or *Incorrect*, and every wrong option names its trap (for example, *scope shift*).
- **Numerical:** the skill being tested, the calculation step by step, and the typical errors that produce the wrong options.
- **Abstract:** the rule or rules in plain words, and exactly why each wrong figure fails.
- **Situational judgement:** the full ranking of all four actions and the competency behind it.
- **Accuracy and prioritising:** where the discrepancy or constraint is, plus a *fast route* for doing it quicker next time.
- **Mock exams:** a diagnostic table after each answer key shows which skill every question tests and which chapter to revisit.

## Study plans
**Four-week plan (about 1 hour a day)**
- **Week 1:** Part I, Chapters 1–3. Verbal questions 1–30 and numerical questions 1–30. Start your error log.
- **Week 2:** Chapter 4. Abstract questions 1–40. Verbal 31–65. Numerical 31–55.
- **Week 3:** Chapters 5–7. Abstract 41–75. Numerical 56–75. Situational judgement, accuracy and prioritising sets if your procedure includes them. Mock Exam 1.
- **Week 4:** Review the error log. Mock Exam 2 early in the week and Mock Exam 3 two days before your test. Light revision only on the last day.

**Eight-week plan (about 30 minutes a day)**
- **Weeks 1–2:** Part I. Foundation questions in every test type, untimed.
- **Weeks 3–5:** Intermediate questions, timed. Weekly review of the error log.
- **Week 6:** Advanced questions. Mock Exam 1.
- **Week 7:** The other test types your procedure requires. Mock Exam 2. Re-attempt every error.
- **Week 8:** Mock Exam 3. Revise your strategy notes, then rest.

# Chapter 1 — EU Selection Tests at a Glance
The European Personnel Selection Office (EPSO) organises selection procedures (often called competitions) for the institutions and agencies of the European Union. Most procedures begin with computer-based multiple-choice tests. Candidates who do well enough in these tests move on to later stages, such as an assessment of their qualifications or an assessment centre.

## Which tests does this book cover?
The core reasoning tests appear across EPSO's generalist administrator (AD) and assistant (AST) procedures, and in many specialist ones:
- **Verbal reasoning:** understanding a text and identifying the statement it supports.
- **Numerical reasoning:** extracting and processing information from tables and charts.
- **Abstract reasoning:** identifying the rule that governs a sequence of figures.

Some procedures, mainly assistant-level (secretaries/clerks, AST-SC), contract-agent (CAST) and certain specialist competitions, have also used other test types:
- **Situational judgement:** choosing the most and least effective response to a workplace scenario.
- **Accuracy & precision:** checking data quickly and without errors.
- **Prioritising & organising:** planning time, tasks and resources under constraints.

**Not covered:** this book does not cover EU-knowledge tests, digital-skills tests, language-comprehension tests or field-specific tests. Use the official sources for those.

> **Always check the Notice of Competition.** Test types, numbers of questions, time limits, pass marks and whether tests are eliminatory differ between procedures and change over time. The Notice of Competition (or call for expressions of interest) for *your* procedure is the authoritative source. This book teaches the skills that transfer across formats.

## Typical format
- **On screen, multiple choice, one answer per question.** Verbal and numerical questions typically offer four options and abstract questions five.
- **Strict time limits.** The test is designed so that time pressure matters. Most candidates do not finish comfortably.
- **No negative marking in typical formats.** A blank answer and a wrong answer usually score the same, so you should never leave a question blank. Confirm this in your procedure's instructions.
- **Your chosen language.** Reasoning tests are generally taken in a language you select from those allowed. This book is in English. The reasoning skills are the same in every language.
- **Test centre or remote.** Tests may be taken at an accredited test centre or remotely under online supervision, depending on the procedure. Both use similar on-screen tools.

## How results are used
Reasoning tests are often combined into a single score or marked against pass thresholds. In some procedures, a high mark in one test can compensate for a lower mark in another; in others, each test has its own minimum. Either way, aim to be solid in **all three** reasoning tests rather than brilliant in one.

> **Pass marks are a floor, not a target.** In competitive procedures, passing is often not enough: only the best-placed candidates go forward. Aim well above the minimum.

## Timing at a glance
These are the timings used for the mock exams in this book. They reflect typical past formats; the Notice of Competition sets the real ones for your procedure.

# Chapter 2 — Verbal Reasoning
In a verbal reasoning question you read a short passage, typically 120–220 words, and choose the one statement that the passage supports. The skill being measured is disciplined reading: separating what the text actually says from what you know, assume or expect.

## The golden rule: only the text counts
Treat the passage as the only source of truth. A statement can be accurate in the real world and still be the wrong answer, because the passage does not say it. Equally, the correct answer may sound surprising, as long as the passage supports it.

> **The test question to ask of every option:** “Where exactly in the passage is this stated or necessarily implied?” If you cannot point to the words, the option is wrong.

> **Coach’s note.** The passage does not care what you know about medieval trade, coral reefs or tax policy. If a fact is not on the screen, it does not exist, however proud of it you are.

## The six classic traps
Most wrong options use one of a small number of traps. Learn to name them and you will spot them quickly.
1. **Extreme wording.** The option says *always, never, all, only, entirely* or *guarantees*, while the passage says *often, some, may* or *in most cases*.
2. **Scope shift.** The passage makes a claim about one group, place or period; the option widens or narrows it (for example, “in coastal regions” becomes “across the country”).
3. **Cause and effect.** The passage reports that two things occur together; the option claims one causes the other, or reverses the direction of cause.
4. **Outside knowledge.** The option is plausible or even true in reality, but the passage never says it.
5. **Partial truth.** Most of the option matches the passage, but one detail — a number, an actor, an order of events, a condition — is wrong.
6. **Contradiction.** The option states the opposite of the passage, often in different words.

Watch for two more advanced variants. *Unsupported comparison* turns a statement about A alone into a comparison between A and B. *Unsupported inference* draws a conclusion that might be likely but is not required by the text. A **proposal is not a decision**, and a **plan is not a result**: be precise about the status of what the passage describes.

## A reliable method
1. **Read the question first** so that you know whether you are looking for the statement that *is* supported or the one that is *not*.
2. **Read the passage once, actively.** Note the main claim, and circle qualifiers (*some, most, may, unless*), numbers and conditions.
3. **Test each option against the text.** For each one, find the relevant sentence and compare the wording closely. Cross out any option you can name a trap for.
4. **Choose the option that survives.** If two seem to survive, re-read the qualifiers: one of them almost always overstates.

## “NOT supported” questions
Some questions flip the task and ask which statement is **not** supported. Three options will then be backed by the passage. Find the sentence for each one and tick it; the option you cannot tick is your answer. Be careful: an option that contradicts the passage and one the passage never mentions are both “not supported”.

> **Speed tip.** Do not try to memorise the passage. Read it for structure, then go back to the exact sentence each option refers to. Most errors come from relying on memory instead of re-checking.

## Common mistakes
- Choosing the option that *sounds* most sensible or most like the main idea, rather than the one the text supports.
- Accepting a paraphrase that quietly changes “may” to “will” or “some” to “most”.
- Spending too long on one passage. Make your best choice, flag it and move on.
- Falling for an option because it is exactly what you would argue at a dinner party. The test is not asking for your opinion.

## Worked example
*A regional authority introduced free bus travel for residents over 65 in three of its five districts. In the first year, bus journeys by older residents in those districts rose by 30%, while their car journeys fell slightly. Officials noted that some of the increase came from trips that had previously not been made at all, such as visits to relatives. The authority has proposed extending the scheme to the remaining two districts, subject to a review of its cost next year.*

Which of the following statements is correct?

- **A.** Free bus travel led older residents in all five districts to drive less.
- **B.** The scheme now covers all five districts.
- **C.** Part of the rise in bus use came from journeys that older residents would not otherwise have made.
- **D.** Car journeys by older residents in the three districts fell by 30%.

**How to solve it.**
- **A — scope shift (and cause and effect).** The scheme ran in three districts, not five, and the passage reports a slight fall in driving without claiming that the scheme caused it.
- **B — a proposal is not a decision.** Extension has only been proposed, and it depends on a cost review.
- **C — correct.** It paraphrases “some of the increase came from trips that had previously not been made at all”.
- **D — partial truth.** The 30% figure belongs to bus journeys; car journeys fell only “slightly”.

# Chapter 3 — Numerical Reasoning
Numerical reasoning questions give you one or more tables or charts and ask a question that requires one to three calculation steps. The maths is rarely beyond lower-secondary level. The difficulty lies in choosing the right numbers, using the right method and working accurately under time pressure. An on-screen calculator is normally available, but check what your procedure allows.

## The essential toolkit
**Percentage of a total.**
Share = part ÷ total × 100. Make sure the “total” is the one the question refers to: a row total, a column total or the grand total.

**Percentage change.**
Change = (new − old) ÷ old × 100. The base is always the *earlier* or *reference* value. A rise from 80 to 100 is +25%; a fall from 100 to 80 is −20%.

**Percentage points versus per cent.**
If unemployment falls from 10% to 8%, it has fallen by 2 **percentage points**, which is a fall of 20 **per cent**. Read the question carefully: it tells you which one it wants.

**Reverse percentages.**
If a value *after* a 25% increase is 500, the original was 500 ÷ 1.25 = 400, not 500 × 0.75 = 375.

**Compound growth.**
Growing at 10% a year for 3 years multiplies a value by 1.1 × 1.1 × 1.1 = 1.331, not by 1.30.

**Weighted averages.**
To average across groups of different sizes, multiply each group's average by its size, add the results and divide by the total size. A simple average of the group averages is a classic trap.

**Ratios.**
Divide both parts by their highest common factor: 45:30 = 3:2. Keep the order the question asks for.

**Index numbers.**
With a base year of 100, the change from an index of 110 to 121 is (121 − 110) ÷ 110 = 10%, not 11%.

**Currency conversion.**
If €1 = 1.50 units of a currency, a price of 300 units is 300 ÷ 1.50 = €200. Divide when converting *into* euros and multiply when converting *out of* euros.

**Rates and time.**
Time = distance ÷ speed. A 20% rise in speed does not cut journey time by 20%.

## Mental-maths shortcuts
| Fraction | Per cent | Fraction | Per cent |
| 1/2 | 50% | 1/6 | 16.7% |
| 1/3 | 33.3% | 1/8 | 12.5% |
| 1/4 | 25% | 1/10 | 10% |
| 1/5 | 20% | 1/12 | 8.3% |

- Multiplying by 1.25 is the same as dividing by 0.8; dividing by 1.25 is the same as multiplying by 0.8.
- A rise of 20% followed by a fall of 20% leaves you 4% down (1.2 × 0.8 = 0.96).
- To find 15%, take 10%, then add half of it.

> **Coach’s note.** Percentages are the most polite liars in the building. A 50% fall followed by a 50% rise does not take you back to where you started; it leaves you 25% down. Make every percentage show you its base.

## A reliable method
1. **Read the question before the data.** Identify exactly what is being asked, including units (thousands? millions? per cent?) and the time period.
2. **Locate the numbers.** Find the precise row, column and year. Many errors are reading errors, not maths errors.
3. **Plan the calculation in words** (“Year 3 minus Year 1, divided by Year 1”), then compute.
4. **Estimate before you calculate.** A rough figure (for example, “a bit over 20%”) lets you eliminate options and catch keying errors.
5. **Check units and rounding** against the options. If two options are close, recompute more carefully.

> **Distractors are built from typical mistakes:** the wrong base, percentage points instead of per cent, a simple average instead of a weighted one, simple growth instead of compound growth. If your answer matches an option, ask yourself whether you made one of these mistakes before you commit.

## Reading charts
- Read the axis labels, units and legend before anything else.
- Where data labels are printed, use them; do not estimate from bar heights.
- With line charts, identify the series by marker shape and line style, not position alone.
- With pie charts, every slice is a share of the stated total; amounts = share × total.

## Worked example: two tables, two steps
Unit A has **40** staff and a training budget of **€600** per person. Unit B has **25** staff and **€840** per person. *By what percentage is Unit B’s total training budget lower than Unit A’s?*

1. **Totals first.** Unit A: 40 × €600 = €24,000. Unit B: 25 × €840 = €21,000.
2. **Change against the right base.** The question compares B with A, so A is the base: (24,000 − 21,000) ÷ 24,000 = 12.5%.

**Traps the options would contain:** 40% (comparing the per-person amounts instead of the totals), 14.3% (dividing by B instead of A) and €3,000 (the difference in euros, not in per cent).

# Chapter 4 — Abstract Reasoning
Abstract reasoning questions show a series of five figures and ask which of five options comes next. Each figure contains one or more elements that change according to a rule. The test measures your ability to spot patterns, form a hypothesis and check it systematically, with no words or numbers to help you.

## What can change
The figures in this book (and in typical tests) are built from a small set of variables:
- **Position:** an element moves around the frame, clockwise or anticlockwise, by a fixed or changing number of positions. The frame has eight positions: the four corners and the four edge midpoints.
- **Rotation:** an element turns by a fixed angle, such as 45°, 90° or 135°, in a fixed direction.
- **Shading:** an element cycles through white, grey and black, or alternates between two of them.
- **Number:** the count of elements rises or falls steadily, or alternates (for example +2, −1, +2, −1).
- **Shape:** a shape gains a side each time, or cycles through a fixed sequence (triangle, square, pentagon, …).
- **Reflection:** an asymmetric shape (such as an F) is mirrored left to right, often in every other figure and combined with a rotation. Compare figures with the same orientation first. Tip: follow the F’s upright stroke (the end that carries the top bar). The F is mirrored across its own upright stroke, so a mirror image never changes which way that stroke points; only the side the bars are on changes. Read the turns straight off the stroke and judge the mirroring separately.

## The rule-hunting checklist
Work through the variables one at a time, for one element at a time.
1. **Isolate one element.** Follow it through all five figures and ignore everything else.
2. **Ask the five questions:** Where is it? Which way does it point? What shading does it have? How many are there? What shape is it?
3. **Describe the change between each pair of figures** (for example, “+2 positions clockwise”) and look for a constant, alternating or steadily growing step.
4. **Predict the sixth figure** for that element only, then eliminate options that disagree.
5. **Repeat for the next element** until one option remains.

> **Elimination is faster than construction.** You rarely need to picture the whole answer. Each rule you confirm usually eliminates one or two options.

## Common patterns and traps
- **Alternating steps:** movements of 1, 2, 1, 2 … or rotations of 45°, 90°, 45°, 90° … Check the step after the last figure carefully.
- **Growing steps:** movements of 1, 2, 3, 4 … positions.
- **Independent rules:** two elements following different rules in the same frame. Treat them separately.
- **Option traps:** the right element in the wrong position, the right position with the wrong shading, or a figure that simply repeats the fifth one.

## Worked example
[[abstract-example]]

**Element 1, the arrow:** it points up, right, down, left, up. That is a 90° clockwise turn each time, so next it points **right**.
**Element 2, the black circle:** it moves one position anticlockwise around the frame each time, so next it sits at the **bottom middle**.
Only **option C** satisfies both rules. A leaves the circle where it was; B over-rotates the arrow; D turns the arrow the wrong way; E moves the circle two positions instead of one.

# Chapter 5 — Situational Judgement
Situational judgement tests (SJTs) present realistic workplace scenarios and four possible actions. You choose the **most** effective and the **least** effective. In procedures that use them, SJTs assess behavioural competencies such as working with others, communicating and delivering quality.

## Typical competency themes
EU selection procedures have long assessed a stable set of competencies. Their exact names vary between procedures, but the themes are typically:
- **Analysing & problem solving:** identifying the facts and finding practical solutions.
- **Communicating:** being clear and precise, and adapting the message to the audience.
- **Delivering quality & results:** taking responsibility and meeting standards and deadlines.
- **Learning & development:** improving your skills and learning from feedback.
- **Prioritising & organising:** focusing on what matters most and planning realistically.
- **Resilience:** staying effective under pressure, change and setbacks.
- **Working with others:** co-operating, sharing information and resolving conflict constructively.
- **Leadership:** guiding and motivating others, and taking ownership. This mainly applies to higher grades.

## How to rate the options
Effective actions in an EU institutional setting tend to be:
- **Proactive and proportionate:** dealing with the problem at the right level, neither ignoring it nor over-escalating.
- **Constructive and respectful:** talking directly to the person concerned before involving others, and protecting working relationships.
- **Service-oriented and rule-aware:** following procedures, meeting obligations to colleagues and stakeholders, and respecting confidentiality.
- **Transparent:** keeping the right people informed early, especially about risks to deadlines or quality.

Ineffective actions tend to avoid the problem, shift blame, break rules or confidentiality, damage relationships, or escalate before trying a reasonable direct step.

> **Answer as an effective colleague would, not as you might under stress.** Think about what the institution would consider best practice. Bear in mind that the most effective option is not always the longest or the most cautious.

## Worked example
*You and your colleague Lena are preparing a briefing for your head of unit, due on Thursday. On Tuesday you notice that Lena’s section still uses last year’s figures. Lena is in meetings all day.*

- **A.** Correct the figures yourself and say nothing.
- **B.** Send Lena a short message flagging the figures and offering to update them if she agrees, so that the briefing stays on schedule.
- **C.** Tell your head of unit that Lena’s section is out of date.
- **D.** Wait until Thursday morning and raise it with Lena then.

**Most effective: B.** It fixes the problem early, respects your colleague’s ownership of her section and protects the deadline. **Least effective: C.** It escalates before you have tried the obvious direct step, and it damages trust. A solves the problem but bypasses Lena, so it is acceptable but weaker. D is late and puts the deadline at risk. **Ranking: B > A > D > C.**

> **Coach’s note.** The heroic option (staying up all night to fix everything yourself and telling nobody) is rarely the best one. Institutions value colleagues who flag problems early far more than colleagues who suffer in silence.

# Chapter 6 — Accuracy & Precision and Prioritising & Organising
These tests have been used mainly in assistant-level and contract-agent procedures. Both reward careful, systematic work under time pressure.

## Accuracy & precision
You compare information, such as reference numbers, names, amounts and codes, against a source table and spot discrepancies.
- **Compare field by field, character by character.** Do not read a code as a word; read it in chunks (for example “TR | 48 | 21 | KX”).
- **Know the usual errors:** transposed digits (4821 → 4812), look-alike characters (0/O, 1/I, 5/S), missing or added accents (Novák → Novak), similar names (Janssen/Jansen), and similar country codes (SI/SK, LT/LV).
- **Use a pointer.** Following the line with a finger or the cursor reduces skipped characters.
- **“No error” is a real option.** Do not invent a discrepancy because you expect one.

> **Coach’s note.** Your brain is a very helpful autocorrect. In this test, that is exactly the problem: it will cheerfully read “Novak” as “Novák”. Slow your eyes down, not your thinking.

## Prioritising & organising
You plan meetings, sequence tasks or allocate work under constraints.
- **Write the constraints down as a list** before looking at the options.
- **For scheduling,** check every required person for each candidate slot, and remember any excluded days and time limits. If the question asks for the *earliest* slot, check slots in time order.
- **For task sequencing,** work out the earliest finish time of each task: its own duration plus the latest finish among its prerequisites. The project length is the longest chain (the critical path), not the sum of all durations.
- **For allocation,** start with the task or person that has the fewest possible options; one forced choice usually unlocks the rest.

## Worked example: the critical path
| Task | Working days | Can start only after | Earliest finish |
| A. Draft | 3 | — | day 3 |
| B. Legal check | 2 | A | day 5 |
| C. Translation | 4 | A | day 7 |
| D. Final approval | 1 | B and C | day 8 |

B and C run in parallel after A, so D can start only when the slower of the two (C) has finished. The project takes **8 days**. Adding all the durations (10 days) is the classic trap: it ignores the work that runs in parallel.

# Chapter 7 — Test-Day Strategy
## Pacing
- **Divide the time by the number of questions** so you know your average budget per question, and check your progress at the quarter, half and three-quarter marks.
- **Don't get stuck.** If a question takes more than about one and a half times your budget, make your best choice, flag it if the system allows, and move on.
- **No blanks.** Where there is no negative marking, answer every question, even if you have to guess in the final seconds.

## Guessing intelligently
Eliminate what you can, then choose from the options that remain. In numerical questions, a quick estimate often eliminates two options. In verbal questions, an extreme or out-of-scope option can usually be crossed out quickly.

## The day before and on the day
- Check the technical and identity requirements in your invitation, especially for remote tests, which may require a system check, a clear desk and a quiet room.
- Know the rules on the calculator, scrap paper and breaks for your sitting.
- Sleep, eat and arrive (or log in) early. A calm start is worth several marks.

> **Coach’s note.** The night before the test is not the moment to try a new technique, a new energy drink or a new box set. Boring preparation produces exciting results.

## After each mock exam
Score it, enter the result in the tracker and spend at least as long reviewing it as you spent sitting it. That review is where the improvement happens. A mock exam you do not review is just a long way of feeling bad about yourself.
